Breaking the Brick Kiln Slave Cycle — One Child at a Time

Across Pakistan, families trapped in generational debt are forced to send their children — some as young as five — to work in brick kilns instead of school. Grace Charity School buys back that debt with something more powerful: an education. Once a child is in our classroom, the cycle breaks with them. The Reality

Some Children Never Get to Be Children

In the brick-kiln communities Grace Charity School serves, poverty doesn't ask permission. Families take on debt just to survive — and that debt gets repaid in labor, sometimes their children's. A child who should be learning to read spends his day stacking wet clay in extreme heat instead, for wages that vanish into a debt that never seems to shrink.

This isn't a rare tragedy. It's Tuesday, for tens of thousands of families across the region. And it keeps happening for one simple reason: no one has offered them another way.

Break the Cycle
The Solution

Grace Charity School Is Proving It Doesn't Have to Be This Way

Since 2010, Grace Charity School has done something simple and stubborn: shown up for the same children, in the same community, year after year. Today, more than 700 kids who would otherwise be working in brick kilns are in a classroom instead — learning to read, eating a real meal, and hearing, often for the first time, that they matter.

PK Christian Sponsors is the U.S.-based partner that keeps this work funded — one sponsor, one child, one month at a time.

Boots on the Ground

The Man Who Wouldn't Look Away

Pastor Victor Sammuel grew up poor himself, one of six children. In 2007, with no funding and no building, he started preaching in the open streets of his own community. When he saw children working brick kilns instead of sitting in classrooms, he couldn't unsee it — so he started a school for them, one family visit at a time.

In 2012, that conviction nearly cost him his life: he was abducted and held for 24 days before he was freed. He didn't stop. Today he leads a school serving hundreds of children who call him more than a principal — to them, he's the reason they get to be kids.
